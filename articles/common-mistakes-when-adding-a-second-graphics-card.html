Common Mistakes When Adding a Second Graphics Card

Most disappointing multi-GPU builds share the same handful of avoidable mistakes, and recognizing them ahead of time can save both money and frustration. The most common is buying extra cards before ever measuring the current system's actual bottleneck.

If your pipeline is already lane-limited, thermal-limited, or dispatch-limited at the CPU level, adding another GPU doesn't fix any of those constraints — it just adds a second device competing for the same limited resources.

Power sizing is another frequent failure point. Builders often size their PSU to bare nameplate minimums instead of leaving margin for sustained load and transient spikes, which creates instability risk right when the system is under the most pressure.

Airflow gets ignored more than it should. Placing cards close together without redesigning case airflow leads directly to throttling, and throttled cards deliver worse real-world performance than a single well-cooled card ever would.

There's also a tendency to assume all workloads scale the same way across multiple GPUs, when scaling behavior is highly specific to the application and task at hand. A full list of these pitfalls — plus how to avoid them — is covered in this guide to multi-GPU planning mistakes.

The fix for most of these issues is simple: test before you buy, and always have a rollback plan.
